03 / THE ETHICAL QUESTION

Who benefits?
Who bears the cost?

AI can create real value. The ethical challenge is deciding who should account for the resources it uses—and whose interests count when businesses scale it.

01

Global benefits.
Local burdens.

A litre of water has different consequences in a dry watershed than in a water-abundant one. Users can be far from the communities supplying cooling water and electricity. Local scarcity matters as well as total volume. [3]

BUSINESS RESPONSIBILITY

Assess local water stress and community needs when choosing infrastructure.

02

Transparency
before comparison.

A cooling-water figure and a life-cycle water figure cover different things. Google and Mistral publish useful data with different boundaries. Comparing their headline numbers as a simple ranking would mislead. [1] [4]

BUSINESS RESPONSIBILITY

Ask suppliers to disclose their methods, boundaries and limitations.

03

Efficiency
and sufficiency.

A more efficient request can coexist with rising total demand if usage grows faster. Task choice also matters: text, image and more intensive applications have different footprints. A text benchmark cannot represent every AI use. [2] [5]

BUSINESS RESPONSIBILITY

Track absolute use, avoid unnecessary generations and match the system to the task.

04 / UNDER THE COUNTERS

An estimate needs
an explanation.

This is a learning tool. Its counters repeat a published reference; they do not measure individual prompts, your device or this website.

Where do the three numbers come from?+

Google’s 2025 study reports a median Gemini Apps text request in May 2025 at 0.24 Wh, 0.26 mL of water and 0.03 g CO₂e (Table 2). This is company-authored research, not an independent audit of every AI provider. Read the original study ↗

What is included—and what is missing?+
  • Electricity: serving accelerators, CPU, memory, idle capacity and data-centre overhead.
  • Water: direct freshwater consumption for cooling. Electricity-generation and manufacturing water are excluded.
  • CO₂e: market-based electricity emissions, plus allocated hardware and direct emissions under the study’s method. It is not a location-based grid reading. CO₂e expresses greenhouse gases as carbon-dioxide equivalents.
  • Outside this reference: training, storage, external networks and user devices. It is not a full life-cycle footprint.

See sections 3.1–3.3 of source [1].

Why do different example prompts add the same amount?+

The examples illustrate everyday uses; they have not been individually measured. Actual resource use varies with model, response length, hardware, location and other conditions. We therefore apply the same clearly labelled text-request reference to each simulated generation, rather than inventing precise costs from its wording.

Image, video and intensive reasoning tasks are outside this simulator’s benchmark. [5]

How are the totals and scenarios calculated?+

Session total = simulated requests × reference per request.
Group scenario = people × daily requests × days × reference per request.

The reference is a median, not a mean. Multiplying it describes a hypothetical set of reference-equivalent requests; it does not predict actual aggregate use. Rounded source values also limit precision. “Repeat 10×” represents ten separate requests.

Units convert automatically: 1,000 mL = 1 L; 1,000 Wh = 1 kWh; 1,000 g CO₂e = 1 kg CO₂e. Water and carbon use their published reference values, rather than being recalculated from electricity using an unsupported conversion factor.

Sources & methodology.

Primary publications behind the numbers and the argument. Each serves a different purpose; their measurements are not interchangeable.

    COUNTER REFERENCE · 2025Elsworth et al. · Measuring the environmental impact of delivering AI at Google Scale ↗

    Company-authored Gemini Apps study. Table 2 supplies all three counter values; sections 3.1–3.4 define scope and the median reference.

  2. 02
    GLOBAL ENERGY CONTEXT · 2026International Energy Agency · Key Questions on Energy and AI ↗

    Source for 485 TWh in 2025 and the 950 TWh central projection for 2030. Both cover all data centres, not AI alone.

  3. 03
    WATER & LOCAL IMPACTS · REVISED 2025Li et al. · Making AI Less “Thirsty” ↗

    Explains water withdrawal versus consumption, direct versus indirect water, geographic differences and water–carbon trade-offs. Its GPT-3 estimates are not used in these counters.

  4. 04
    ACCOUNTING & TRANSPARENCY · 2025Mistral AI · Our contribution to a global environmental standard for AI ↗

    Company-published life-cycle assessment summary, conducted with Carbone 4 and ADEME and externally reviewed. Provides a different accounting perspective; not a like-for-like comparison or counter input.

  5. 05
    TASKS & MODEL DIFFERENCES · FACCT 2024Luccioni, Jernite & Strubell · Power Hungry Processing: Watts Driving the Cost of AI Deployment? ↗

    Empirical energy research across tasks and models. Supports the limits of applying a single text-generation reference to all AI uses.
